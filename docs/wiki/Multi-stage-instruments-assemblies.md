# 10. Multi-stage instruments (assemblies)

A real instrument is rarely one field. A funnel at a few torr hands ions to a multipole at millitorr; an orthogonal accelerator pushes a packet into a mirror. Each region wants its own grid, its own pitch, its own gas and its own clock — forcing them into one solve would mean solving the whole instrument at the finest pitch and the highest pressure found anywhere in it. An assembly instead keeps each stage its own solved model and flies ions across declared seams.

The assembly document is JSON like everything else: a list of stages — each carrying a complete specification of its own, a pose placing it in the world (offset and rotation), and an exit plane where it hands the ion to the next stage — plus a beam block saying what is flown. The shipped example is examples/funnel_hexapole/instrument.json: an RF funnel posed into a tilted-wire hexapole, with a detector at the hexapole's exit. Notebook 11 works it end to end through the API.

Seams are checked, loudly, before anything flies. A handoff is supposed to be field-dead: the preflight measures the worst residual field an ion can see at each seam and states the number as an advisory — quantified, never blocking. A declared bound that sits inside its own stage's exit plane is refused outright: every ion would terminate on the bound before reaching the seam, so the handoff is dead by construction.

Each stage flies on its own terms. Its clock is its own declared maximum flight time — a timeout names the stage whose clock expired, and raising another stage's does nothing. Its recording density and buffer are its own. Its gas is its own, so a gas-filled funnel feeds a vacuum analyzer without either compromising. Its stations and bounds are authored in its own frame, exactly as when the stage is flown alone. What crosses the seam is the ion's state: position, velocity, time.

The beam is generated, not pasted. The usual declaration is from_stage: the packet is produced from the named stage's own source block at load, so the instrument flies the same births that stage would fly alone. The ion-count field in the application then narrows that packet for a quick look — an even stride across the declared distribution, never just its head — or, when the packet carries its generator's stamp and seed, regenerates it at a larger count from the same declaration. Duplicating rows is refused: two identical ions fly identical paths and would double-count every histogram.

What comes back is counted by one rule (section 9): detected / flown. The status line reads, for example, "70/75 detected", followed by the named reasons for the rest — hit metal in the funnel, left the hexapole's box, timed out — and an assembly with no declared detector says so instead of printing a number: ions were flown, nothing measured them. Time-of-flight statistics and resolving power are computed from detected ions only, per mass; a spread pooled across masses would dress mass separation up as peak width, so the headline refuses to pool it.

Before the first ion flies, the same record-volume guard that protects a single-stage flight prices what an assembly flight will retain — the drawn trajectory subset — against your declared memory budget, refusing with the arithmetic and the levers when it cannot fit (section 11).

In the application, the Load tab's Deck \> Multi FA sub-tab drives all of this: paste or load the instrument JSON, pick which stage the view and the flight parameters address, and Fly assembly launches the solve-and-fly on a worker thread with progress streaming to the status line — the first 3-D solve compiles kernels and can take minutes cold while the application stays live. The View pane draws every stage posed in the world frame with the seams marked, and the 3-D Flight Viewer (section 13.2) shows the flown paths inside the full instrument.
